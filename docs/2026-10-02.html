## THE BIG THING ### Amazon Is Trying to Get $8 Billion in Nvidia Chips Off Its Books — and That Should Worry You Amazon is exploring a deal to shift approximately $8 billion worth of top-end Nvidia chips off its balance sheet, according to the Financial Times, as reported by Bloomberg Technology. The structure being explored would likely involve a sale-leaseback arrangement or a third-party financing vehicle — mechanisms that let a company retain operational use of assets while transferring ownership risk to another party. This isn't a minor accounting maneuver. When the world's largest cloud provider starts engineering ways to avoid carrying $8 billion in GPU inventory as a direct liability, you're looking at a signal about how Amazon's leadership actually feels about near-term AI infrastructure demand versus what it says publicly. The context here is critical. Amazon, Microsoft, and Google collectively announced over $300 billion in combined AI infrastructure capex for 2025 and beyond, with AWS committing to aggressive GPU procurement to close the perceived gap with Microsoft Azure's head start on AI workloads via its OpenAI partnership. AWS has been simultaneously pushing its own custom silicon — Trainium and Inferentia chips — as a cost-competitive alternative to Nvidia's H100s and H200s, making an $8 billion Nvidia position somewhat awkward strategically. Carrying that inventory at full book value while simultaneously arguing customers should move to your own chips is a difficult story to tell investors. The FT's reporting does not identify a counterparty to the proposed deal, and the specific financial structure remains unconfirmed. The broader implication is one the market has been nervously circling for months: hyperscaler GPU demand may be softening at the margin, or at minimum, the internal economics of holding high-depreciation AI hardware are becoming harder to justify on a straight balance-sheet basis. Nvidia's data center chips depreciate rapidly as newer generations arrive — the H100 is already being displaced by the B200 — meaning an $8 billion position can deteriorate in value faster than traditional infrastructure assets. If Amazon successfully executes this kind of off-balance-sheet vehicle, expect Microsoft and Google to face immediate investor pressure to justify their own GPU carrying costs. It also hands ammunition to analysts who have argued that hyperscaler capex announcements reflect procurement commitments and competitive signaling more than genuine, near-term monetizable demand. What to watch: First, whether a counterparty — likely a specialized infrastructure fund or sovereign wealth vehicle — is identified, which would confirm the deal's feasibility and reveal who actually wants to hold Nvidia GPU risk right now. Second, watch Amazon's Q3 earnings commentary on AWS capex trajectory; any downward revision would corroborate the read that this is demand-driven caution rather than pure financial engineering. Third, track Nvidia's response — if this deal closes, it complicates the "insatiable demand" narrative that has supported Nvidia's valuation multiple. The FT report has not been independently confirmed by Amazon or Nvidia as of this writing.
