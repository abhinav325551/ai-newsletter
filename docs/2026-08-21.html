## The Big Thing: Every Built an AI Clone of Its Editor-in-Chief — and It Actually Works Every CEO Dan Shipper has done something that cuts to the heart of a question most media companies are quietly terrified to ask: what happens when you train an AI on the accumulated editorial judgment of your best editor? In an interview with Platformer's Casey Newton, Shipper reveals that Every built an AI agent modeled on editor-in-chief Nathan Baschez using roughly 30,000 copyedits as training data. The result isn't a chatbot that mimics tone — it's a system that attempts to replicate *editorial decision-making* at scale. Shipper is simultaneously doubling headcount and automating core editorial functions, which tells you something important: this isn't a cost-cutting story, it's a capability-expansion story, and that distinction matters enormously for how you think about AI's near-term impact on knowledge work. The context here is critical. Every has been one of the most intellectually serious publications covering AI productivity, which means Shipper isn't experimenting blindly — he's operationalizing ideas his own writers have been analyzing for years. Training on 30,000 copyedits represents a genuinely novel approach to capturing tacit knowledge. Most editorial style guides fail precisely because good editing is intuitive and contextual, not rule-based. If Every has found a method to extract and systematize that intuition from behavioral data rather than explicit instruction, that's a meaningful technical and organizational insight, not just a product demo. Shipper also acknowledges what he calls the "dirty secret" of writing with AI — a phrase Newton doesn't fully unpack in the excerpt, but which likely refers to the uncomfortable reality that AI-assisted prose is becoming indistinguishable from unassisted work, raising authenticity questions that the industry is conspicuously avoiding. The broader implication is that *editorial identity* — long considered the moat of premium media — is now partially replicable. If you can compress a senior editor's judgment into an agent trained on their behavioral output, you've changed the economics of scaling a publication without degrading quality. That's a direct challenge to the assumption underpinning every high-end newsletter and media brand: that the human at the center is irreplaceable. It also accelerates a bifurcation already underway — between media organizations that will use AI to extend genuine human expertise, and those that will use it to replace expertise they never actually had. Every, given Shipper's Anthropic adjacency (his fiancée works there, a conflict he discloses), is building primarily on Anthropic's models, which means Claude's constitutional AI approach is the substrate for these editorial agents. That's worth tracking as a data point on enterprise adoption. Watch for two things. First, whether other editorial brands attempt to replicate this approach — the 30,000-copyedit training set is a high bar, requiring years of documented, granular editorial behavior that most publications haven't preserved. Second, watch how Every's readers respond as AI-assisted and agent-generated content becomes more prevalent in the product. Shipper is being more transparent than most, but the industry norm is opacity, and reader trust is the variable that could make or break this model. If Every publishes its methodology in any detail, it will become the template everyone else quietly copies.
