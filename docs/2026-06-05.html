## The Big Thing: Nvidia Locks In the Full HBM4 Supply Chain Jensen Huang confirmed on the record for the first time that Nvidia has certified all three major memory manufacturers — Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron — to supply HBM4 for its Vera Rubin AI accelerator platform, according to Bloomberg Technology. This is not a minor procurement update. HBM4 represents the next generational leap in memory bandwidth for AI training workloads, and Vera Rubin is Nvidia's post-Blackwell architecture expected to push the company's datacenter dominance further into the latter half of this decade. Huang's explicit confirmation of all three suppliers simultaneously signals that Nvidia is aggressively securing redundancy at the top of its memory stack — a direct lesson learned from the HBM3e supply constraints that throttled H100 and H200 shipments in 2023 and 2024. The context here matters enormously. SK Hynix has held a structural advantage in HBM supply, being the first to qualify for HBM3e on the H100 and capturing a disproportionate share of early Blackwell allocations. Samsung, by contrast, has faced well-documented yield and quality issues with its HBM3e production, and its qualification for Blackwell-era chips came later and with caveats. Micron entered HBM3e production as a credible third source but at lower volumes. Certifying all three for HBM4 simultaneously — rather than sequentially — suggests Nvidia has pressure-tested the supply architecture and is building in competitive tension between suppliers from day one, which should structurally improve Nvidia's negotiating position on pricing and delivery timelines. For the broader AI infrastructure landscape, this has cascading implications. Hyperscalers and cloud providers — the ultimate customers downstream of Nvidia — have been burned by GPU allocation delays tied to memory bottlenecks. A three-supplier HBM4 ecosystem reduces single-point-of-failure risk and, in theory, accelerates the ramp of Vera Rubin deployments. It also puts meaningful pressure on AMD and Intel: if Nvidia locks in preferential HBM4 capacity early across all three fabs, competing accelerator platforms face a tighter available supply window for their own next-generation chips. Memory bandwidth has been the quiet constraint shaping the entire AI compute race, and Nvidia just moved to dominate that constraint rather than be victimized by it. Watch three things from here. First, whether Samsung's HBM4 qualification is full and unconditional or comes with volume limits — the company's HBM yield history warrants scrutiny, and a conditional certification would concentrate real supply back toward SK Hynix. Second, how this affects Micron's valuation and capex guidance; investor consensus on Micron's HBM trajectory has been volatile, and a confirmed Vera Rubin design win is a material data point. Third, when AMD discloses its own HBM4 supply arrangements for its next Instinct generation — the gap or parity between Nvidia's and AMD's certified supplier lists will tell you a great deal about who controls the memory allocation queue heading into 2026 and 2027 datacenter build cycles.
