## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: AI's Semiconductor Windfall Is Now a Macroeconomic Problem

The Bank of Korea issued a formal warning this week that the AI-driven semiconductor boom is creating inflationary pressure that extends well beyond the tech sector. According to Bloomberg, the central bank flagged that soaring bonuses at major South Korean technology firms — fueled by surging demand for AI chips — risk triggering broader wage growth across the economy and stoking stronger consumer demand. This is no longer a story about chip stocks or datacenter buildout. It's a story about AI reshaping monetary policy decisions in one of the world's most semiconductor-dependent economies.

The context matters here. South Korea's economy is structurally intertwined with semiconductor exports to a degree few others are — Samsung and SK Hynix together dominate global HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) production, the memory architecture that Nvidia's H100 and B200 series depend on almost entirely. As hyperscalers have accelerated AI infrastructure spending through 2025 and into 2026, Korean chip revenues have surged, and with them, the compensation packages at the firms producing those chips. When engineers and fab workers at companies of that scale receive exceptional bonuses, wage expectation spillovers into adjacent industries are a well-documented phenomenon in Korean labor economics. The BOK isn't speculating — it's pattern-matching against domestic inflation dynamics it has navigated before.

What makes this analytically significant for you is that it represents a second-order effect of AI capital expenditure that almost no one was modeling 18 months ago. The conventional framing was that AI spending would pressure GPU supply chains and energy grids. The BOK's warning suggests the monetary transmission mechanism runs further: hyperscaler capex → Korean fab revenue → executive and worker bonuses → wage norm resetting → broad CPI pressure → rate policy friction. If the BOK is forced to hold rates higher for longer to contain this dynamic, it introduces a cost-of-capital headwind for Korean tech firms at precisely the moment they're being asked to expand HBM capacity aggressively. That's a tension with real supply implications for the global AI buildout.

Watch two things closely. First, whether other semiconductor-heavy economies — Taiwan most obviously, but also the Netherlands given ASML's position — begin flagging similar wage-inflation dynamics in central bank communications. If this becomes a regional pattern, it reframes AI infrastructure spending as a source of macroeconomic instability in key supplier nations, which is a variable that neither the Fed nor major AI investors are currently pricing. Second, watch how Samsung and SK Hynix structure compensation going forward — if they shift toward equity-heavy packages to manage cash bonus exposure, that tells you the BOK's warning landed internally and that Korean chipmakers are already trying to thread the needle between retaining talent and avoiding regulatory and monetary blowback.
