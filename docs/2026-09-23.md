## 1. The Big Thing

# The Big Thing: Heidi's $900M Valuation Signals AI's Deepening Grip on Clinical Infrastructure

Australia's Heidi has closed a $100M funding round at a $900M valuation, cementing its position as one of the most heavily backed AI healthcare startups outside the United States. CEO and co-founder Thomas Kelly spoke to Bloomberg about the raise, framing Heidi's core proposition around reducing administrative friction for physicians — specifically targeting clinical documentation, research synthesis, and patient consultation workflows. The company is Melbourne-based, which makes this valuation notable: a sub-unicorn AI health company built outside the traditional Silicon Valley or London funding ecosystems, now apparently competitive with players in those markets on pure capital terms.

The context here matters. Healthcare has been one of AI's most contested verticals precisely because the failure modes are consequential and the regulatory surface is enormous. Most early AI health plays focused on diagnostics or drug discovery — areas with cleaner evaluation metrics. Heidi is betting on the operational layer instead: the documentation burden, the note-taking, the clinical workflow scaffolding that consumes an estimated 30-50% of a physician's working hours according to multiple health system studies. This is the same space where Nabla, Abridge, and Nuance's DAX Copilot (now embedded inside Microsoft's health cloud) are competing, meaning Heidi is entering a market that already has well-capitalized incumbents and a Microsoft-backed player with existing EHR distribution.

What's strategically interesting is the geographic arbitrage. Heidi appears to be building market density in Australia and New Zealand first — regulatory environments with centralized health systems that are arguably easier to navigate than the fragmented U.S. payer landscape — before pushing into larger markets. Kelly's comments about expansion plans suggest this isn't a company content to remain a regional player, but the sequencing is deliberate. If you're an investor or a competing founder, you should read this as a playbook: establish clinical credibility in a high-trust, lower-noise market, generate outcomes data, then use that as the wedge into the U.S. or U.K. That's a slower burn than going straight for U.S. health systems, but potentially a more defensible one.

Watch two things from here. First, whether Heidi pursues or announces any EHR integration partnerships — without hooks into Epic or Oracle Health, clinical workflow AI remains a parallel system rather than infrastructure, which caps both utility and stickiness. Second, watch how U.S.-based competitors respond to a well-funded offshore player with a differentiated go-to-market; if Heidi starts announcing U.S. health system pilots in the next 12-18 months, expect acquisition conversations to surface quickly. At $900M, it's still within plausible acqui-hire or strategic acquisition range for a major health tech platform.
