## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: AI's Power Hunger Is Now Reshaping National Energy Policy

South Korea's government announced it will actively pursue ways to compress nuclear power plant construction timelines to meet surging electricity demand driven by AI infrastructure buildout, according to Bloomberg Technology. This isn't a minor policy tweak — it's a sovereign government reorienting its long-term energy strategy around the operational requirements of data centers and AI compute clusters. When a country starts restructuring its nuclear permitting and construction apparatus to serve AI demand, you're watching the technology reshape national infrastructure policy in real time.

The context matters here. South Korea already operates one of the world's most advanced nuclear programs — its APR-1400 reactor design has been exported to the UAE — and its state utility KEPCO has been under pressure as domestic power demand accelerates. Korean hyperscale data center construction has intensified significantly, driven both by domestic AI investment and by global cloud providers seeking stable, relatively low-cost power in Northeast Asia. Traditional construction timelines for nuclear plants run 10-15 years in most Western countries, though South Korea has historically built faster. The government's signal that even those timelines need compressing tells you something about how acute the demand forecasts look internally.

The broader implication is that AI's energy problem is graduating from a corporate real estate challenge to a geopolitical and infrastructure one. What began as hyperscalers quietly locking up natural gas capacity and signing long-term renewable PPAs is now prompting governments to make multi-decade capital commitments on the assumption that AI compute demand will remain structurally elevated. South Korea is not alone — the U.S., UK, France, and Japan have all made nuclear-forward energy announcements with explicit AI demand framing in the past 12 months. But South Korea's move to specifically accelerate construction timelines, rather than simply reaffirming existing capacity, signals a more urgent operational posture. For investors, this is a meaningful data point: governments are now treating AI infrastructure demand as a planning certainty, not a variable.

Watch two things closely. First, whether South Korea moves to streamline its regulatory review process — that's where most construction time actually lives, and any compression there would be the substantive story. Second, watch whether Korean nuclear construction expertise becomes a geopolitical export asset; KEPCO and Doosan have the industrial capacity that most Western nations lack, and accelerated domestic programs tend to sharpen that competitive edge further. If Korean construction timelines drop meaningfully, expect other AI-intensive economies to study that model closely.
