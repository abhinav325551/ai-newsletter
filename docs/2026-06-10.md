## 1. The Big Thing

## THE BIG THING

### ASML Is the Most Undervalued Chokepoint in AI Infrastructure — And the Market Is Only Just Noticing

ASML hit fresh record highs this week, but the more important signal is buried beneath the headline: the Dutch lithography monopolist is trading at its cheapest forward price-to-earnings ratio relative to major semiconductor peers in over a decade, according to Bloomberg's Dasha Afanasieva reporting from Amsterdam. That disconnect matters because ASML isn't a peripheral player in the AI buildout — it is the singular, non-substitutable supplier of extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines required to manufacture leading-edge chips at 7nm and below. Every NVIDIA H100, every custom AI ASIC from Google, Amazon, and Microsoft, every TSMC wafer feeding the current AI supercycle runs through machines that only ASML can produce. A company with that level of structural leverage trading at a relative valuation discount isn't a curiosity — it's an anomaly demanding explanation.

The context here is critical. ASML's recent performance has been shaped by two countervailing forces: genuine demand acceleration driven by hyperscaler AI capex commitments running into the hundreds of billions, and persistent overhang from U.S. export controls that have progressively restricted ASML's ability to sell even older DUV (deep ultraviolet) systems into China — historically a significant revenue contributor. The Dutch government, acting under U.S. pressure, has curtailed ASML's China export licenses since 2023, and Washington has continued tightening those restrictions. That regulatory risk has weighed on sentiment even as the underlying Western demand picture strengthened materially. Investors appear to be discounting ASML for the China exposure it's losing rather than pricing the monopoly infrastructure position it retains everywhere else.

The strategic implications for the AI landscape are significant and underappreciated. The entire AI infrastructure narrative has been dominated by discussion of model companies, GPU allocations, and hyperscaler spending — but the actual physical constraint on AI scaling sits one layer deeper, at the fab equipment level. TSMC cannot simply spin up new leading-edge capacity on demand; each new fab requires years of lead time and a queue of ASML EUV tools that the company produces at a rate of roughly 20 high-NA units per year at full ramp. If you're modeling the ceiling on AI compute availability over the next three to five years, ASML's production throughput is a harder constraint than NVIDIA's packaging capacity or TSMC's wafer starts. The relative valuation discount suggests institutional money hasn't fully internalized this bottleneck dynamic, or is still anchoring to the China revenue haircut as the dominant variable.

What to watch: ASML's next earnings call for any revision to EUV shipment guidance and backlog figures — the backlog number is the most honest leading indicator of where leading-edge semiconductor capacity is heading 18–24 months out. Also watch for any executive commentary on high-NA EUV adoption timelines; Intel, TSMC, and Samsung are all in early-stage deployment of high-NA tools, and acceleration there would be a direct signal that the next node transition is pulling forward. Finally, monitor U.S.-Netherlands diplomatic channels around export control scope — any further restrictions on DUV shipments to China could trigger another sentiment reset, while any relaxation (unlikely under current policy trajectory) would be an immediate catalyst. The valuation gap Bloomberg is flagging is either a genuine mispricing or a rational discount for geopolitical risk that isn't going away. Determining which is the right frame is the most important analytical question in semiconductor infrastructure right now.
