## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: SAP's AI Bet Is a Referendum on Whether Legacy Enterprise Software Survives the Decade

SAP CE Christian Klein is wagering the company's future on an AI pivot that will determine whether the German ERP giant remains relevant or becomes the next cautionary tale of a category leader disrupted from below. Bloomberg's profile this week reveals a CEO acutely aware that SAP's core business — selling expensive, deeply embedded enterprise software to the world's largest companies — faces existential pressure from AI-native competitors who are rebuilding the ERP stack from scratch, without 50 years of technical debt. That Klein is personally evangelizing the transformation, quite literally jumping out of BMWs to make the pitch, signals that the urgency inside SAP is real, not performative.

The context matters here. SAP sits at the center of global business infrastructure in a way that few technology companies do — its software touches roughly 87% of global commerce by transaction volume, running supply chains, financial systems, and HR processes for companies like Walmart, ExxonMobil, and Siemens. That embedded position is both SAP's greatest asset and its deepest vulnerability. Enterprise customers don't rip out SAP systems lightly, which historically gave the company pricing power and retention. But AI is beginning to change the calculus: startups like Cohere, Glean, and a growing cluster of vertical AI agents are demonstrating that specific enterprise workflows can be automated or replaced without touching the underlying system of record — they route around SAP rather than through it, quietly eroding the value layer that justifies SAP's licensing costs.

The implications for the broader AI landscape are significant. SAP's struggle is a leading indicator for the entire legacy enterprise software category — Oracle, Salesforce, Workday, and ServiceNow all face versions of the same problem. If SAP can credibly embed AI into its core workflows and prove measurable ROI to its installed base, it validates the "incumbent retrofit" thesis: that large software vendors can absorb the AI transition by distribution advantage alone. If it fails — if customers begin unbundling workflows to AI-native alternatives — it accelerates a platform shift that would be the largest restructuring of enterprise software spending since the cloud migration of the 2010s. Investors are already pricing in the uncertainty; SAP's stock has outperformed European tech peers, but that premium reflects hope, not demonstrated AI revenue at scale.

Watch three things specifically: First, SAP's Business AI revenue figures in its next earnings call — Klein needs to show that AI features are driving net new contract value, not just retention. Second, whether major SI partners like Accenture and Deloitte are building AI practices *on* SAP's stack or quietly building migration paths *away* from it; their incentive structures will telegraph where enterprise spending actually flows. Third, keep an eye on whether any of SAP's largest reference customers — the marquee names Klein trots out in investor presentations — publicly attribute productivity gains to SAP's AI tooling with specific, auditable metrics. Testimonials without numbers are a red flag that the breakthrough Klein needs is still more aspiration than product.
