## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: China's AI Hardware Divorce From Nvidia Is Accelerating

A new Bloomberg survey of Chinese technology companies confirms what the export control debates have long foreshadowed: Chinese firms are actively migrating off Nvidia hardware and onto domestic alternatives at scale. This isn't the story of a few state-owned enterprises making politically motivated procurement decisions — it's a broad-based industrial shift that signals the export restriction strategy may be producing the opposite of its intended effect. Rather than starving China's AI buildout, the controls have catalyzed a domestic semiconductor ecosystem with genuine commercial momentum. The survey doesn't specify exact adoption figures, but the directional signal from Bloomberg's sourcing is unambiguous: Nvidia is losing Chinese customers not just because it can't legally sell them H100s and H800s, but because local alternatives have become viable enough that companies are choosing them.

The context here is layered. Nvidia's China revenue had already cratered following the Biden administration's October 2022 and October 2023 export control rounds, which restricted sales of A100, H100, and later H800 chips. Nvidia attempted to thread the needle with downgraded variants — the A800 and H800 — but those too were eventually blocked. The result was a forced incubation period for Chinese chipmakers. Huawei's Ascend 910B and 910C chips have emerged as the primary beneficiaries, with CXMT and Cambricon also capturing portions of the accelerator market. ByteDance, Baidu, and Alibaba have all reportedly increased Ascend procurement. The critical development is that Chinese hyperscalers have had enough time — roughly two years of constrained Nvidia access — to retool their software stacks, including rewriting CUDA-dependent workloads for domestic hardware. That software transition cost is what previously made switching prohibitively expensive; it now appears largely absorbed.

The implications for the broader AI infrastructure landscape are significant and cut in multiple directions. For Nvidia, China was historically a revenue contributor in the low-to-mid teens as a percentage of data center sales before controls tightened — losing that market permanently is a material headwind, though Jensen Huang has publicly downplayed it. More strategically, if Chinese firms successfully scale on Huawei silicon, they reduce the leverage that chip controls were designed to provide. Washington's export control apparatus was premised on a dependency that is visibly eroding. For the rest of the world, this survey is also a proof point that the AI accelerator market is no longer a Nvidia monoculture — a finding that will be closely watched by sovereign AI programs in the EU, India, and the Middle East that are similarly anxious about infrastructure dependency on a single American vendor.

Watch three things closely from here. First, Huawei's ability to scale Ascend production — TSMC is out of the picture, so SMIC's 7nm-equivalent yields are the binding constraint, and any evidence of manufacturing breakthroughs would be a major signal. Second, whether the U.S. responds with additional controls targeting software, tooling, or memory chips (HBM is still a chokepoint since SK Hynix and Samsung are restricted from selling to sanctioned Chinese entities). Third, watch Nvidia's next earnings call for any revision to its China revenue guidance or commentary on the competitive dynamic — Huang's framing of domestic Chinese competition has historically been dismissive, and a tone shift would be telling.
