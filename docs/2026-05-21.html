## The Big Thing: The AI Money Loop Is Now a Global Macro Story The circular economy of AI spending has officially escaped Silicon Valley and become a worldwide capital flow story. Bloomberg Technology reports that Asian chipmakers — led by the likes of TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix — are generating windfall profits from the insatiable demand for AI hardware, and that money is not sitting idle. It's cycling back into the global economy as capital expenditure, infrastructure investment, and — critically — reinvestment into the very hyperscalers driving the original demand. What you're watching is a self-reinforcing loop that now operates at a geopolitical scale: Nvidia sells GPUs, TSMC prints money manufacturing them, TSMC and its suppliers spend that money on equipment and capacity, and portions of those profits flow back into the tech ecosystem as investment, deposits, and procurement contracts that ultimately fund more AI buildout. This mirrors, on a macro level, what analysts have been tracking within the U.S. AI ecosystem for the past two years — the so-called "circular AI boom" where Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon pour hundreds of billions into infrastructure, Nvidia captures the margin, and Nvidia's cash flows back into the market in ways that sustain valuations and further investment. The difference now is geographic and systemic: Taiwan's export revenues, South Korea's semiconductor earnings, and Japan's materials and equipment suppliers are all becoming nodes in this loop. TSMC alone reported record revenue in early 2025, driven overwhelmingly by advanced node demand for AI accelerators. That capital is now influencing currency flows, sovereign investment decisions, and regional industrial policy in ways that central banks cannot ignore. The implications for the broader AI landscape are significant and underappreciated. First, this dynamic makes the AI buildout structurally harder to slow down — the financial interests now embedded across multiple Asian economies mean there are governments and central banks with a stake in continued hyperscaler spending, not just VCs and tech executives. Second, it concentrates geopolitical risk in ways that should concern anyone modeling long-term AI infrastructure: the loop runs directly through the Taiwan Strait. Any disruption to TSMC's output doesn't just affect chip supply — it now threatens a capital recycling mechanism that funds AI development globally. Third, it raises serious questions about whether current GPU valuations and hyperscaler capex projections are being stress-tested against a genuine demand correction, or whether the circularity of the money flow is masking the absence of an external revenue anchor. Watch three things from here. First, whether sovereign wealth funds and central banks in Taiwan, South Korea, and Japan begin explicitly acknowledging AI hardware revenue as a factor in monetary or fiscal planning — that would signal the loop has become institutionally recognized, not just analytically observed. Second, track TSMC's capital expenditure guidance for 2026 and beyond; if they're investing windfall profits into new fabs predicated on continued hyperscaler demand, the loop tightens further and the systemic risk compounds. Third, and most importantly, watch for any hyperscaler that blinks on capex — Microsoft, Google, or Amazon signaling a slowdown would send a contractionary signal that now reverberates well beyond Nasdaq, landing on currency desks and trade ministries across Asia.

## Models & Research This week's signal cuts in two directions: on the capability frontier, OpenAI is making its boldest mathematical claim yet — with independent mathematicians now in the loop — while Cohere quietly dropped one of the most practically significant open-weight enterprise models in months. Meanwhile, arXiv output suggests the field is converging on two under-discussed problems: *why* iterative reasoning models actually generalize, and *how* to stop wasting compute on inefficient diffusion decoding.
- **[TechCrunch] OpenAI claims a new reasoning model has disproved an Erdős geometry conjecture first posed in 1946** — Unlike the October false alarm when then-VP Kevin Weil prematurely declared GPT-5 had solved 10 open problems, OpenAI is this time involving external mathematicians for verification, which is the correct prior for taking the claim seriously rather than dismissing it outright. [Link](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/20/openai-claims-it-solved-an-80-year-old-math-problem-for-real-this-time/) - **[VentureBeat] Cohere released Command A+, a 218-billion-parameter model under a full Apache 2.0 license** — The combination of lossless quantization and native citations in a genuinely permissive license is a direct competitive move against Meta's Llama ecosystem for regulated enterprise deployments where output provenance is non-negotiable. [Link](https://venturebeat.com/technology/cohere-cracks-lossless-quantization-and-native-citations-with-first-full-apache-2-0-licensed-open-model-command-a) - **[arXiv] "Equilibrium Reasoners" proposes that iterative latent-state models generalize because they learn task-conditioned attractors — stable fixed points corresponding to valid solutions** — This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not yet a proved theorem, but it offers the first structured framework for explaining *why* test-time compute scaling via iterative updates works beyond the training distribution. [Link](http://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21488v1) - **[arXiv] Fixed-Point Distillation (FPD) collapses multi-step discrete diffusion image generation into a single forward pass** — Prior single-step distillation approaches either doubled compute via auxiliary score networks or required fragmented multi-stage pipelines; FPD's fixed-point formulation avoids both failure modes, which matters considerably for inference cost at scale. [Link](http://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21484v1) - **[arXiv] New analysis of hyperparameter transfer finds that the embedding layer learning rate is a systematically underweighted variable in LLM training scaling laws** — The finding directly challenges the assumption that μP-style parameterizations make *all* hyperparameters scale-invariant, with practical implications for labs running expensive scaling experiments on miscalibrated search spaces. [Link](http://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21486v1) - **[Bloomberg] Andrew Dai, former Google DeepMind researcher, is building Elorian, a visual reasoning AI startup, and discussed hiring and funding plans at the JPMorgan Global China Summit** — Few technical details are public at this stage, but Dai's background in multimodal reasoning at DeepMind makes Elorian worth tracking as the visual reasoning segment becomes increasingly contested.
