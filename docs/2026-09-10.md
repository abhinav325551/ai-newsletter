## 1. The Big Thing

## THE BIG THING

### DOJ Opens Antitrust Investigation Into Nvidia-Groq Licensing Deal

The Justice Department has opened a formal investigation into Nvidia's $20 billion licensing agreement with AI chip startup Groq, according to Bloomberg Technology. The core concern: whether Nvidia deliberately structured the deal as a licensing arrangement — rather than an acquisition — specifically to sidestep the merger review thresholds that would trigger mandatory HSR filings with federal regulators. This is not a routine inquiry. A licensing deal of this magnitude between the dominant AI chip supplier and one of its most credible inference-chip rivals is exactly the kind of arrangement that antitrust enforcers have flagged as a structural evasion tactic in the current technology environment.

To understand why this matters, you need the context. Nvidia controls an estimated 70–80% of the AI accelerator market by revenue, and Groq has emerged as one of the few serious challengers to that dominance — not on training workloads, but on inference, where its Language Processing Unit architecture delivers deterministic, low-latency throughput that CUDA-based GPUs struggle to match at cost. A $20 billion licensing tie-up between those two players is not a routine IP transaction. It creates financial interdependence between Nvidia and the startup most likely to erode its inference margin, without triggering the formal merger review process that would require both parties to produce documents and submit to regulatory scrutiny. The DOJ appears to be asking whether that outcome was a feature, not a coincidence.

The broader implication is significant for the entire AI infrastructure deal landscape. Regulators under the current administration have already signaled heightened scrutiny of Big Tech's preferred investment structure — the "strategic partnership" that confers control without triggering merger law. Microsoft-Inflection, Google-Anthropic, and Amazon-Anthropic all drew varying degrees of regulatory attention for similar reasons. If the DOJ successfully recharacterizes Nvidia's Groq arrangement as a de facto acquisition — or establishes that the licensing structure was designed to obscure competitive harm — it creates a legal precedent that could unwind or restructure how hyperscalers and chip companies approach every major AI infrastructure deal going forward. Investment bankers and M&A counsel at every major AI company are reading this filing carefully right now.

Watch three things: first, whether the DOJ issues a Civil Investigative Demand to Nvidia, which would force document production and signal the investigation is advancing toward formal action; second, how Groq's existing customers — particularly inference-heavy deployments at enterprise scale — respond to the uncertainty, since a forced restructuring of the deal could affect Groq's capitalization and roadmap; and third, whether this investigation emboldens the FTC or international regulators (particularly the EU's DG COMP) to open parallel proceedings. Nvidia's stock has been the single largest driver of S&P 500 returns over the past 18 months — a sustained antitrust overhang on its most strategically important partnerships is a material risk that the market has not yet fully priced.
