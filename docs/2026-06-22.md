## 1. The Big Thing

## THE BIG THING

### NVIDIA's 45°C Cooling Threshold Could Reshape the Economics of AI Infrastructure

NVIDIA has announced that its upcoming Rubin-generation AI servers will support liquid cooling at temperatures up to 45°C — a threshold that sounds mundane until you understand what it unlocks. Most data center cooling systems run significantly colder, requiring energy-intensive chillers to maintain safe operating temperatures. At 45°C, Rubin infrastructure can reject heat directly to facility water loops or even ambient air in many climates, eliminating or dramatically reducing chiller dependency entirely. According to NVIDIA's blog, Rubin is the world's first AI infrastructure generation to achieve this specification, and the efficiency gains compound fast: chillers can account for 30–40% of a data center's total energy consumption, meaning this isn't an incremental improvement — it's a structural cost reduction.

The context here matters. As AI clusters scale toward gigawatt-class deployments, cooling has quietly become one of the binding constraints on the entire industry. GPU compute has scaled faster than the infrastructure surrounding it, and operators running dense GPU racks — particularly GB200 NVL72 systems dissipating hundreds of kilowatts per rack — have been forced into expensive, complex direct liquid cooling buildouts regardless. NVIDIA has been pushing its partners toward liquid cooling for two generations now, but the Rubin announcement goes further by raising the *output* temperature of that liquid, which is the variable that actually determines whether you need additional cooling equipment downstream. This is an engineering detail with billion-dollar facility implications.

For you as a founder or investor watching AI infrastructure, the implications cut several directions simultaneously. Hyperscalers building their own data centers — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta — gain negotiating leverage over cooling equipment vendors and potentially accelerate their own construction timelines in geographies previously considered thermally marginal. Colocation providers face a more complicated picture: existing facilities built around traditional air-cooled or chilled-water architectures may need costly retrofits to fully exploit Rubin's efficiency profile, widening the gap between purpose-built AI factories and legacy converted facilities. Liquid cooling specialists like Vertiv and Coolit, which have benefited from the GPU density wave, will need to watch whether NVIDIA's higher temperature tolerance reduces the addressable market for their most complex (and most profitable) cooling solutions.

Watch for two things in the near term. First, how quickly NVIDIA's ODM and hyperscaler partners publish Rubin facility specifications — the actual PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) claims will either validate or complicate the efficiency narrative. Second, whether competitors respond: AMD's MI400-series roadmap and Intel's Falcon Shores both face the same thermal physics, and a meaningful gap in cooling efficiency between NVIDIA and its rivals would add yet another dimension to NVIDIA's infrastructure moat beyond raw compute performance. The Rubin generation isn't shipping yet, and full technical specifications beyond what NVIDIA has disclosed remain limited — treat the 45°C headline as a directional signal confirmed by NVIDIA, with operational proof points still to come.
