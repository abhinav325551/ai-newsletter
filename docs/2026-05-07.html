## The Big Thing **The White House Just Told Every AI Lab It's On Its Own** White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles stated publicly that the Trump administration will not pick winners and losers in the AI race — and if you're reading that as a neutral, hands-off posture, you're missing the second-order effects. Coming from the person who controls access to the Oval Office and shapes the daily policy agenda, this isn't a throwaway line. It's a signal that the administration is actively resisting pressure — almost certainly from multiple competing lab lobbying operations — to designate a national AI champion the way the U.S. effectively did with semiconductor policy and the CHIPS Act. That pressure has been building since OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and others have each made their respective cases in Washington for preferential treatment in federal contracts, export licensing frameworks, and compute access. The context here matters. The Biden administration's AI Executive Order, revoked by Trump in January 2025, had already begun laying the groundwork for a government-mediated AI safety regime that critics argued implicitly advantaged incumbents with the compliance infrastructure to absorb new reporting requirements. Trump's early moves — dissolving the AI Safety Institute's leadership, pulling back on mandatory safety disclosures — signaled deregulatory intent. Wiles' statement now adds a competitive neutrality doctrine on top of that deregulation posture. The combination is significant: less regulatory burden *and* no government thumb on the scale. That's a different policy cocktail than either pure laissez-faire or industrial policy, and it has real distributional consequences across the lab ecosystem. The implications cut in several directions at once. For smaller and mid-tier labs — xAI, Mistral's U.S. partnerships, Cohere, and others — a neutrality posture is structurally favorable; it forecloses the scenario where a single incumbent gets entrenched via federal preferment. For OpenAI specifically, which has been aggressively courting the administration and recently restructured its corporate governance partly to remain viable as a government contractor, this is a more complicated message. The Stargate infrastructure deal — a $500 billion compute buildout announced with Trump in January — already created the appearance of winner-selection, and Wiles' statement may be an attempt to manage that perception before formal AI policy directives land. It also complicates the export control picture: a "no winners" doctrine sits in tension with any policy that grants specific companies preferred status in selling AI infrastructure to allied nations. Watch for the actual policy directives Wiles referenced as forthcoming — those documents will reveal whether "no winners" is a genuine operating principle or a rhetorical hedge. Specifically, look at how federal AI procurement rules are written: if contracts are structured around capability benchmarks rather than named vendors, the neutrality claim holds. If they're written around security clearance infrastructure that only two or three labs currently possess, it doesn't. Also watch Anthropic's and OpenAI's next moves in Washington; both have invested heavily in government relations operations premised on a different assumption about how this administration would behave. The lobbying calculus just shifted.
