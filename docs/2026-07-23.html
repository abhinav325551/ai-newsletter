## The Big Thing: Southeast Asia Is Becoming the Quiet Center of Global AI Infrastructure The numbers out of Thailand this week are striking enough to reframe how you should be thinking about AI infrastructure geography. Foreign investors filed applications to invest 1.37 trillion baht ($40.6 billion) in Thailand in the first half of 2026 alone — an 80% year-over-year surge, according to Bloomberg Technology — with AI data centers driving the bulk of that capital. To put that in perspective: Thailand is pulling in more declared FDI intent in six months than many mid-sized economies attract in a decade. This isn't a blip. It's a structural reorientation of where the physical layer of AI gets built. The context matters here. Southeast Asia has been quietly assembling the preconditions for this moment for several years: relatively cheap land and power, governments actively competing on incentive packages, improving subsea cable connectivity, and — critically — geographic positioning outside the direct blast radius of US-China tech decoupling. Companies building global AI infrastructure need sovereign diversification. They can't put everything in Virginia or Singapore, the latter of which has already imposed data center moratoriums due to power constraints. Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia have stepped into that vacuum aggressively. Malaysia in particular has already attracted hyperscaler commitments from Microsoft, Google, and Amazon; Thailand's surge suggests the investment wave is now broadening beyond the obvious first mover. The implications for the broader AI landscape are twofold. First, this confirms that the compute buildout remains nowhere near its ceiling — capital is still flooding into physical infrastructure at an accelerating pace, which has direct downstream effects on GPU demand, power equipment suppliers, and cooling technology vendors you should be tracking. Second, and more strategically, it signals that AI capability is becoming genuinely multipolar at the infrastructure level before it becomes multipolar at the model level. The country or region that hosts the data centers accrues real leverage: jobs, energy contracts, data residency rights, and eventual regulatory authority over what runs on that hardware. Thailand's government understands this, which is why its Board of Investment has been fast-tracking approvals. What to watch: whether Thailand's FDI applications convert to actual shovels in the ground at a comparable rate — application volume and disbursement are different animals, and power grid capacity remains a genuine bottleneck across the region. Watch also for which hyperscalers and sovereign wealth funds are anchoring the largest deals when disclosures emerge; the composition of that investor list will tell you whether this is primarily a US-company-driven buildout or whether Gulf capital and Chinese firms (operating through intermediaries) are quietly claiming positions. The geopolitical subtext of who owns AI infrastructure in a non-aligned region is a story that will matter enormously by the end of the decade.

## SaaS Disruption Watch The week's signal is unmistakable: AI isn't creeping into SaaS categories at the margins anymore — it's replacing headcount and compressing entire product categories into API calls. Uber's customer service cuts are the template, not the exception. As commodity model prices collapse (Laguna S 2.1 now undercuts DeepSeek V4 Flash while outperforming V4 Pro on benchmarks), the unit economics that once justified standalone SaaS tooling for scheduling, ticketing, and content ops are evaporating in real time.
--- - **[Bloomberg Technology] Uber cuts 10% of customer service workforce, explicitly attributing the reduction to AI adoption** — This is direct displacement of the customer support SaaS stack: Zendesk, Intercom, Salesforce Service Cloud, and Freshdesk all derive substantial revenue from per-seat licensing tied to human agent headcount; as Uber and peers automate tier-1 and tier-2 resolution, the seat count driving those contracts shrinks structurally, not cyclically. [Link](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/uber-cuts-10-of-customer-service-jobs-citing-embrace-of-ai) - **[OpenAI Blog] OpenAI publishes case studies of news organizations deploying ChatGPT for reporting, content accessibility, and advertiser relations** — This puts direct pressure on CMS-adjacent SaaS incumbents — specifically SEO optimization tools (Clearscope, MarketMuse), content workflow platforms (Contently, Cision), and personalization layers (Piano, Arc XP) — by collapsing their discrete functions into a single model-layer integration that news orgs are now doing in-house via OpenAI's API. [Link](https://openai.com/index/how-news-organizations-are-using-ai) - **[Latent Space] Laguna S 2.1 benchmarks above DeepSeek V4 Pro at roughly 10x smaller scale and lower cost than DeepSeek V4 Flash** — Continued price-performance compression at the model layer is the mechanism killing verticalized AI-SaaS middleware: any startup that built a moat on "we fine-tuned a large model for your use case" and charges a SaaS margin on top is now being undercut by neolab models that are simultaneously cheaper and more capable, a dynamic that threatens players like Writer, Cohere, and sector-specific LLM wrappers across legal, HR, and finance. [Link](https://www.latent.space/p/ainews-laguna-s-21-released-cheaper) - **[Bloomberg Technology] OpenAI's models breached Hugging Face's internal systems in hours — a task Bloomberg's sources say would have taken a skilled human attacker far longer** — This directly pressures the cybersecurity SaaS category, but the mechanism cuts both ways: it validates AI-native security challengers like Simbian and Dropzone AI over legacy SIEM/SOAR platforms (Splunk, IBM QRadar, Palo Alto's XSIAM) that were built around human analyst throughput assumptions, while simultaneously raising the threat baseline those same platforms must defend against.
