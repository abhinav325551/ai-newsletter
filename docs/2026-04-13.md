## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: Compute Is Now a Strategic Resource, Not Just Infrastructure

The most important shift happening in AI right now isn't a new model release — it's a fundamental reframing of what compute *means*. Ben Thompson at Stratechery is arguing, drawing on Doug O'Laughlin's January 2025 Fabricated Knowledge post, that the rise of reasoning models like o1 has collapsed the old ceiling on model improvement. The constraint is no longer architectural — it's economic. If you can spend infinitely, you can improve infinitely. That sounds like good news until you realize what it actually implies: every dollar of compute is now a strategic choice with a measurable opportunity cost, and most organizations are not treating it that way.

The context here matters. O'Laughlin's original provocation was that reasoning models effectively invalidate Aggregation Theory — Thompson's own foundational framework — because they suggest AI capabilities are less about who controls distribution and more about who controls the compute stack at inference time. Thompson's response, framed through the concepts of "Mythos" and "Muse," appears to be wrestling with what replaces Aggregation Theory as the organizing logic of the AI economy. The specific content of those concepts isn't fully surfaced in the available source material, but the framing signals a serious attempt to build a new analytical layer on top of the raw "compute = capability" insight that O'Laughlin introduced.

The implication for the broader landscape is significant. If compute is the binding constraint and the relationship between spend and output is effectively monotonic, then the competitive dynamics of AI stop looking like software and start looking like energy or semiconductor manufacturing — industries defined by capital intensity, supply chain control, and state-level strategic interest. This reframes what Nvidia's market position actually means, why Microsoft, Google, and Amazon are racing to build their own chips, and why sovereign AI infrastructure is becoming a serious policy conversation across the EU, Middle East, and Southeast Asia. You're not watching a software war anymore. You're watching a resource allocation war.

What to watch: Whether Thompson's Mythos/Muse framework gains traction as a replacement mental model among investors and founders — it would signal that the strategic vocabulary of AI is shifting away from "who has the best model" toward "who is making the best compute allocation decisions." Also watch for O'Laughlin's follow-up work at Fabricated Knowledge on the economics of inference scaling, and pay attention to how frontier labs discuss compute budgets in their next round of public disclosures. Any lab that starts publishing inference cost curves rather than benchmark scores will be telling you something important about where the competitive frontier has moved.
