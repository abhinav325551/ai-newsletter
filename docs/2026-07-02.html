## THE BIG THING ### The AI Chip Rally Is Cracking — and the Contagion Is Going Global The semiconductor selloff that began in U.S. markets has now spread to South Korea's Kospi, signaling that what looked like isolated profit-taking may be something more systematic. South Korean chipmakers — most critically Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, which together dominate global HBM (high-bandwidth memory) supply — are caught directly in the blast radius. HBM is the memory architecture that makes high-performance AI inference and training possible at scale, meaning this isn't a peripheral story about speculative tech stocks. It's a stress test on the physical supply chain underpinning the entire AI buildout. The context here matters. AI-linked chip stocks have been on an extraordinary run, driven almost entirely by the narrative that hyperscaler capex — from Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta — would sustain semiconductor demand indefinitely. That narrative encouraged valuations that priced in years of flawless execution. What Bloomberg's reporting now reflects is the market beginning to question the timing mismatch: hyperscalers are spending aggressively, but revenue models that justify that spending at the application layer remain thin, delayed, or unproven. When the gap between infrastructure investment and monetization widens, equities that priced in the optimistic scenario correct — sometimes sharply. The Korean market's involvement is analytically significant because it closes a loop. U.S. chip names like Nvidia carry obvious AI valuation premiums, so their selloffs can be read as sentiment-driven. But South Korean exposure is more fundamental — SK Hynix in particular is Nvidia's primary HBM3E supplier, and Samsung is fighting to qualify its own HBM for Nvidia's next-generation platforms. When those stocks fall in sympathy, you're watching the market reprice not just enthusiasm but actual near-term order expectations. That's a different and more serious signal. Watch three things from here: first, whether the selloff is arrested by any hyperscaler earnings guidance or capex commitment that reasserts demand conviction — the next major test is earnings season. Second, whether SK Hynix's HBM qualification pipeline for Nvidia's Blackwell successor remains on schedule, since any slip there would validate the bearish read. Third, watch whether this spreads further into TSMC's Taiwan-listed shares, which would confirm that the market is repricing the entire AI semiconductor stack rather than just memory. If TSMC holds, this may be a rotation. If it doesn't, you're looking at a broader recalibration of how markets are valuing the AI infrastructure thesis.
