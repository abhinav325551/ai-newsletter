## 1. The Big Thing

## The Big Thing: Washington's AI Hardware Squeeze on China May Be More Bark Than Bite

The Biden-era export control playbook is getting a potential sequel. According to Bloomberg Technology, the US is weighing a ban on Chinese data center components — the kind of hardware that underpins AI training and inference workloads at scale. China's state-run press has already issued warnings against expanding tech curbs, signaling that Beijing views the move as a direct provocation rather than a routine trade adjustment. The timing is particularly fraught: this comes against the backdrop of a fragile trade truce between the two countries, one that both sides have been careful not to visibly rupture since tariff negotiations produced a tentative détente.

The context here matters. The US has spent the better part of three years constructing an increasingly elaborate architecture of AI-related export controls — starting with advanced GPU restrictions in October 2022, tightened again in 2023, and expanded further with the Biden administration's diffusion rules in early 2025. Each successive round has attempted to close loopholes that Chinese firms, most notably Huawei and its HiSilicon division, have exploited through third-country routing and domestic substitution. The proposed component ban represents yet another layer, targeting not just chips but the broader data center supply chain, including networking equipment and memory. China has responded to each prior round not by capitulating but by accelerating domestic alternatives — a pattern you should expect to continue regardless of what Washington decides here.

The critical analytical point, which Bloomberg makes explicit, is that the ban is **unlikely to materially hurt China's export engine**. This is the part that tends to get lost in the geopolitical framing. China's AI hardware exports and its broader manufacturing competitiveness are not primarily dependent on the specific components the US is targeting. Chinese firms have already diversified supply chains, built domestic alternatives for many critical components, and established commercial relationships — particularly across Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa — that don't route through US-controlled technology. The ban would impose real friction and some cost increases, but framing it as a decisive blow to Chinese AI capacity overstates American leverage at this particular layer of the stack.

What you should watch: First, whether the measure advances past the proposal stage at all — it faces opposition from US semiconductor and hardware firms who would lose Chinese customers and face retaliatory procurement restrictions. Second, how China's "official newspaper" warnings translate into concrete countermeasures; Beijing has a documented pattern of using state media signaling as a precursor to targeted economic retaliation against specific US industries. Third, and most consequentially for the AI landscape, watch Huawei's Ascend chip adoption curve among Chinese AI labs. Every American restriction that creates supply uncertainty for NVIDIA H-series alternatives in China accelerates Ascend's domestic market share — a dynamic that, over a 24-to-36-month horizon, could produce a genuinely competitive second hardware ecosystem that operates entirely outside US export control jurisdiction.
